🌊 Fluids!

🤔 Before we delve deeper into the properties of fluids, let's discuss what a fluid even is!

💡 A fluid is any material that can continously move and deform under an applied shear stress or external force.
They have zero shear modulus, or basically, don't resist any shear shear force applied to it.
What... do those words mean?
Let's say you have two buckets. One bucket is filled with water and the other is filled with rocks.

If we were to tip both buckets to a flat surface with no obstacles, at the same time, what do you think will happen?
The first one might look like this, but less dramatic.
We can imagine, that at some point, the rocks will soon stop tumbling and eventually come to rest.
The water on the other hand might continue to flow, and flow, until it is super thin, and then stop.

This is what it means for fluids to continously flow, which means it moves without stopping every now and then.
What's even cooler is that as it was flowing, it changes its shape (by becoming thinner), which we call continous deformation.
This is what sets fluids apart from non-fluids!
Unlike the rocks which can only change shape when it hits the ground hard, which is not all the time, the water can also change its shape while it is moving!

Plus, something extra: If I poke my hand into a tub of fluid, it won't bite back! 😁

Viscosity

⭐ Viscosity is the property of a fluid which offers resistance to the movement of one layer of the fluid over another adjacent layer of the fluid.
⭐ When the two layers of a fluid at a distance "dy" apart, move one over the other at different velocities, say u and u+du.
⭐ The viscosity together with relative velocities causes a shear stress acting between the fluid layers.
Okay! But how would I explain this to a 5-year old?
We can explain it like this: Let's say you have a stack of papers on your desk, neatly arrange into a tall pile.
You suddenly decide to move this pile across the desk using only your breath.

✨ And so you did.
Not as crazy as this of course! hehe
You might have seen that the top pages flew off really quickly, the ones at the middle moved slowly, while the ones at the bottom stood still.
In other words, some layers moved faster than others.

The same thing also happens to layers of fluids! This is the effect of viscosity!
Accha!~ 😄
⭐ The top layer causes a shear stress on the adjacent lower layer while the lower layer causes a shear stress on the adjacent top layer.
⭐ This shear stress is proportional to the rate of change of velocity with respect to y. It is denoted by symbol τ (tau).
τ ∝ du/dy
τ = μ⋅du/dy
⭐ Where μ is the constant of proportionality and is known as the coefficient of dynamic viscosity or only viscosity. du/dy represents the rate of shear strain or rate of shear deformation or velocity gradient.
⭐ From the above equation, we have μ = τ/(du/dy)
⭐ Thus, viscosity is also defined as the shear stress required producing unit rate of shear strain.
Translating into 5-year old speech!! 😄
🏃 Let's go back to the pages analogy!
🕊️ When the top-most page flies off, it drags the page beneath it with it too. On the other hand, the page beneath it clings it from flying off.
🌟 There is a stronger drag if the top-page is a lot faster than the one beneath it.
🌟 The strength of the drag can also be boosted depending on what fluid is being used.
